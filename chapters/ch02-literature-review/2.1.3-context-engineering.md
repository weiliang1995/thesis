#### 2.1.3 Context Engineering

Consider the email example from the previous section, in which the intended audience, tone, and writing constraints are already specified. Suppose the email is intended to provide a supervisor with a project progress update. To produce an accurate update, the LLM would also need information about the work completed, previously agreed objectives, outstanding issues, and the next steps. These details may be known to the person making the request but are not necessarily available to the model. Even when the instruction clearly communicates the intended outcome, the model may still lack the information needed to produce a response that reflects the actual situation. The challenge is therefore not only how to communicate what the model is expected to do, but also how to provide the information required to do it.

Context engineering addresses this broader challenge through the systematic selection, organisation, and management of information supplied to an LLM during inference (Mei et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2025). In an LLM-based agent, this information may include system and user instructions, retrieved knowledge, tool descriptions and results, conversation history, and records of earlier decisions. While prompt engineering primarily focuses on designing inputs that guide model behaviour, context engineering places greater emphasis on managing the information available to the model as the task progresses. The distinction concerns their primary focus rather than a strict separation between the two approaches. As discussed in the previous section, prompt engineering can also involve incorporating external knowledge and examples (Liu et al., 2026). Similarly, Mei et al. (2025) include prompting techniques within their broader context engineering framework. The two approaches can therefore complement each other when designing an LLM-based agent.

Although access to relevant information is important, supplying more information does not necessarily enable the model to perform a task more effectively. An LLM has a finite context window, and its ability to use information within that window may vary with the length and composition of the input. Liu et al. (2024), in experiments involving multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval, found that model performance often declined when relevant information was positioned in the middle of a long input rather than near its beginning or end. These findings illustrate that the capacity to accommodate information is distinct from the ability to use it effectively. In an agent, context also changes over time: as described in Section 2.1.1, each tool action may produce an observation that informs subsequent decisions. Repeatedly retaining all such observations may introduce redundant information and increase input token consumption, while indiscriminately removing earlier information may discard details that are still needed. The challenge is therefore to balance the availability of relevant information against the limitations and resource requirements of maintaining it throughout execution (Anthropic, 2025).

Mei et al. (2025) distinguish three foundational components of context engineering: context retrieval and generation, context processing, and context management. The first concerns obtaining or constructing information relevant to the task. For example, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) retrieves relevant material from an external information source and incorporates it into the model input to support generation. An agent may also retrieve information on demand, such as reading a particular file when further investigation is required. Context processing concerns how available information is prepared for use, for example by organising findings into a structured representation or selecting relevant portions of a larger document. Context management addresses how information is maintained under context and resource constraints over successive interactions. Representative mechanisms include context compaction, which summarises earlier interactions to reduce their input footprint, and external memory, which stores information beyond the current context window for later retrieval (Anthropic, 2025). Information stored externally is not necessarily part of the model's current context until it is retrieved and supplied to the model. These mechanisms address related problems and may overlap in practice; compression, for example, can be understood as both a processing operation and a management strategy.

In software engineering, context requirements may change as a development task progresses. In the login issue described in Section 2.1.2, an agent may initially need the reported behaviour and authentication architecture, then inspect source files and error logs during investigation, and later use regression test results to assess a proposed change. Empirical studies illustrate both the potential benefits and limitations of providing additional repository information. Zhang et al. (2023) evaluated iterative repository-level retrieval for code completion and reported improvements over a baseline restricted to the current file. In a study of coding agents, Lulla et al. (2026) reported shorter median execution times and lower output token consumption when repository-level AGENTS.md files were provided. Gloaguen et al. (2026), however, found that repository-level context files did not generally improve task success and increased inference costs on average in their evaluated settings. These findings concern different tasks, configurations, and outcome measures; reduced output token consumption, for instance, does not necessarily imply lower overall inference cost. They suggest that the effects of context provision should be assessed in relation to the task, the information supplied, and the outcome being measured rather than assumed to be uniformly beneficial.

Nevertheless, providing appropriate context does not by itself ensure that an agent performs the actions required to complete a development task. In the login example, an agent may have access to the authentication architecture, implementation constraints, and regression test cases, yet still conclude its work without running the relevant tests. An instruction to run regression tests before completion can guide the agent's behaviour, but it differs from a programmatically enforced condition that prevents the workflow from being marked complete until the required tests pass. The latter concerns how execution is controlled, how observations are evaluated, and how the system responds to unsuccessful actions. Context engineering can contribute to these activities, and its mechanisms may form part of the environment that supports an agent. However, addressing the execution process also involves considerations such as tool coordination, verification, corrective feedback, and stopping conditions. These considerations motivate the discussion of harness engineering in the following section.
