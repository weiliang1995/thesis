#### 2.2.2 Existing Approaches and Empirical Evidence

Research on AI-assisted development has reported different efficiency effects, partly because the studies examine different forms of assistance and different units of work. In a controlled experiment, Peng et al. (2023) asked developers to implement a JavaScript HTTP server; participants with GitHub Copilot completed the task 55.8% faster than those without it. Paradis et al. (2025) evaluated three AI features with 96 Google engineers performing a standardised development task in the company's environment. Their preferred adjusted estimate suggested approximately 21% less time with AI, although its confidence interval was wide and included no effect. These experiments show that coding assistance can reduce time on the tasks studied. Their comparisons concern access to AI features, however, rather than different ways of coordinating the same coding agent over the course of ordinary development work.

The direction of the effect is not uniform. Becker et al. (2025) randomly assigned 246 real issues from mature open-source repositories to AI-allowed or AI-disallowed conditions. The 16 experienced contributors took an estimated 19% more self-reported implementation time when AI was allowed. The measure included work before and after pull-request review. In the subset of issues with coded screen recordings, developers in the AI-allowed condition spent less time actively coding and more time prompting, waiting for, and reviewing AI output. This activity pattern helps describe where time went in that setting, but it does not establish which activity caused the overall slowdown. The developers primarily used Cursor Pro with Claude models; neither condition tested an additional orchestration workflow around a native Claude Code baseline. Taken with Peng et al. (2023) and Paradis et al. (2025), the findings caution against extrapolating a speed effect from one tool, task, or development environment to another.

Other studies address how agent work is organised. Xia et al. (2024) proposed Agentless, which structures issue resolution into localisation, repair, and patch validation. In the reported SWE-bench Lite evaluation, it resolved 96 of 300 issues with a mean model cost of $0.70 per issue. This demonstrates that a staged approach can perform competitively in a benchmark without relying on a fully autonomous action loop. More recently, Fan et al. (2026) compared planning, tool interfaces, and context-management strategies across four models and benchmark settings. They found that the value of these arrangements depended on the model and the available context budget: planning, for example, could help a weaker model complete more work while acting mainly as a cost-saving aid for stronger models. These evaluations support examining workflow design as an empirical variable. Their success rates and computational costs do not directly measure developer coordination time or the elapsed time required to complete an enterprise development task.

Human participation changes what an efficiency measure captures. Chen et al. (2026) compared GitHub Copilot and the OpenHands agent in a controlled, within-participant study of 20 students. For correctly completed tasks, average active user time was 25.1 minutes with Copilot and 12.5 minutes with OpenHands. The authors estimated an average of approximately 27.9 minutes for OpenHands if the time the agent spent taking actions was included. This illustrates how the developer's active involvement may decrease while overall task time stays similar. The study compares tools with different capabilities, and its short, time-limited tasks do not represent a comparison of two workflows using Claude Code.

Studies of development in larger settings provide another perspective, but often use output rather than per-task time. Song et al. (2024, revised 2026) analysed GitHub Copilot use in collaborative open-source projects and reported increased code contributions alongside an increase in coordination time. That coordination concerns work among contributors rather than developer-agent orchestration. Murphy-Hill et al. (2026) studied an enterprise rollout of Claude Code and GitHub Copilot CLI and estimated that adopters merged approximately 24% more pull requests over the study window. This is particularly relevant evidence that native command-line agents can support productive development work. Merged pull-request volume, however, does not show how long an individual issue takes or whether imposing additional workflow rules around the same agent would change that time.

Table 2.2 compares where each measure starts and ends, or what it counts. The contrast between active time and elapsed time is particularly relevant here because waiting can occur within the developer-owned stage. Pull-request throughput describes output over a period, while the lead-time measure in Section 2.2.3 follows an individual Jira task.

*Table 2.2. Measurement boundaries in selected studies of AI-assisted development.*

| Outcome | Study and comparison | What the measure covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bounded task time | Peng et al. (2023), Copilot access for a JavaScript task. Paradis et al. (2025), AI feature access for a standardised enterprise task. | Time for a prescribed exercise under controlled conditions. |
| Issue implementation time | Becker et al. (2025), AI allowed or disallowed on real open-source issues. | Developer-reported implementation time, including work before and after pull-request review. |
| Active user time | Chen et al. (2026), Copilot versus OpenHands on short tasks. | Participant time on correctly completed tasks. The reported figure including agent actions is a separate estimate. |
| Benchmark resolution and model cost | Xia et al. (2024) and Fan et al. (2026), alternative agent arrangements on issue benchmarks. | Issues solved under benchmark checks and model expenditure per attempt or task. |
| Pull-request throughput | Murphy-Hill et al. (2026), adoption of command-line coding agents in an enterprise rollout. | Pull requests merged over the observation window. |

Together, these studies establish that coding tools and structured execution can affect particular forms of task performance, human effort, and output. They also show why those findings cannot be converted into a single estimate of delivery efficiency: a benchmark resolution rate, minutes of active user effort, implementation time, and pull requests merged each describe a different outcome. The evidence motivates a direct comparison of how developer-owned tasks progress under the two workflows defined in this study.
