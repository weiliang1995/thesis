#### 2.4.2 Existing Approaches and Empirical Evidence

The sources of consumption described in Section 2.4.1 suggest that resource management can operate at several points in agent execution. A system may change the information provided per request, preserve earlier work to avoid rediscovery, alter the architecture of task execution or restrict the number of interactions. The empirical literature provides examples in which these choices reduce expenditure, improve task outcomes or alter the balance between them.

**Context selection and compression.** Context policies determine which observations remain available as execution progresses. Lindenbauer et al. (2025) compare an unmanaged SWE-agent with two policies: masking older tool observations and replacing earlier interactions with model-generated summaries. In their Qwen3-Coder 480B configuration, mean instance cost fell from $1.29 for the unmanaged agent to $0.61 with masking and $0.64 with summarisation, with solve rates of 53.4%, 54.8% and 53.8%, respectively (Table 1). These differences in solve rate were not established as significant; both cost reductions were. This example shows that a comparatively simple policy can achieve resource savings without a demonstrated loss in benchmark performance. It does not show that removing observations is appropriate for every task or that a native agent already using compaction would achieve the same savings.

The accounting of cached context changes the interpretation of such interventions. Yang et al. (2026) separately consider cached input, uncached input and output across more than 20,000 coding-agent runs. In their main retrieved-code experiments, read and search operations decreased by 18–34%, but cost increased by 33–42% under a pricing scenario in which cached input cost one tenth of uncached input, without a measurable improvement in resolution. Removing older observations also produced different cost effects under different cache prices. A shorter trajectory or smaller prompt is therefore not by itself evidence of monetary savings. The pricing scenario and effects on later actions both matter.

**Memory and state reuse.** Rather than discard all earlier work, an agent can carry a concise representation of findings into a subsequent session. KC and Budathoki (2026) compare takeover from the repository alone with handoffs that provide additional context. Across three successor models, context-bearing handoffs reduced median agent events by 20–59% and cumulative prompt tokens by 42–63% relative to repository-only takeover. Effects on resolution were model-dependent. This directly supports the possibility that retaining useful state reduces rediscovery, but the reported takeover savings cannot establish the net cost of generating and maintaining memory throughout an entire development task. Nor does it imply that every handoff should contain a full trace. The relevant design question is what information a later step needs and whether the cost of preserving it is justified.

**Execution architecture and workflow complexity.** Agentless provides a useful alternative to unrestricted autonomous execution. Xia et al. (2024, v2) organise issue resolution into localisation, patch generation and validation, limiting the model's responsibility for deciding the next activity. The approach resolved 96 of 300 SWE-bench Lite issues at a mean model cost of $0.70 per issue. Its performance relative to the open-source agents included in the study challenges the assumption that more autonomous execution is always necessary for effective repair. Agentless is itself a structured procedure, however, not an absence of orchestration. The result does not demonstrate that simpler enterprise tasks require no planning or validation, or that one architecture is optimal across task types.

A more direct assessment of harness components is provided by Fan et al. (2026). They evaluate 176 settings spanning four models, two benchmarks, multiple context-window budgets and component ablations. For example, on SWE-bench Verified at 128k context with the Nemotron-3 30B model, planning enabled produced a 25.2% success rate at $0.09 per task, compared with 13.6% at $0.02 without planning. In contrast, with the 550B model, the corresponding results were 65.8% at $2.33 and 67.8% at $3.31. The latter accuracy difference was not statistically significant in the reported matched comparison. Planning thus bought a substantial accuracy improvement for one model but was associated with lower expenditure and similar accuracy for another. This illustrates why the necessity of a component cannot be inferred from its existence or complexity alone. The study holds other components fixed, unlike a comparison of complete workflows.

**Task complexity and adaptive resource allocation.** The amount of work required by a development issue may differ substantially. For instance, a local wording change may be completed after finding a component and checking the modified behaviour, whereas a change to authentication shared by several applications may require dependency investigation, coordination and broader verification. These are illustrations, not measured complexity thresholds. If a workflow applies the same preparatory steps to every issue, those steps may represent a larger share of the work for a limited change. Conversely, a seemingly local change can have a hidden dependency, so its initial simplicity need not imply that additional verification is unnecessary.

Gao and Peng (2026) provide evidence for adapting execution resources during a task rather than granting every task an identical maximum effort. They compare unrestricted execution, fixed turn limits with reminders, and a dynamic strategy that permits an extension on demand, using 100 SWE-bench Verified tasks and three models. Limits based on the 75th percentile of the unrestricted turn distributions reduced expenditure by 24–68% with small changes in solve rates in the reported settings. Dynamic extensions reduced cost by a further 12–24% compared with the evaluated fixed-limit approaches while achieving comparable or better solve rates. These results support examining whether an execution budget should be adjustable, rather than assuming that more turns are invariably useful. The evaluated turn budgets are not equivalent to the correction-loop limit or the estimated development-duration categories used in this thesis.

There is also evidence against relying on human-assessed difficulty as a precise budget predictor. Bai et al. (2026) find only a weak relationship between expert-rated task difficulty and actual token usage, and no consistent increase in accuracy at higher observed token expenditure. Their analysis suggests that model choice and the unfolding execution trajectory can matter substantially. Because more difficult or unsuccessful runs may both take longer and consume more tokens, an association between expenditure and success cannot by itself establish the causal effect of providing additional inference budget. Together with the turn-control study, this motivates considering task complexity while remaining open to adaptation based on evidence encountered during execution.

The comparisons within each row retain the study's own baseline and resource definition. The numbers should not be used to rank methods across rows, because tasks, models, prices, resource budgets and verification protocols differ.

The table reveals distinct forms of trade-off. In some settings, resource reduction occurs without a statistically established deterioration in success, as in the masking comparison. In others, additional expenditure accompanies a performance improvement, as in the planning comparison for the 30B model. There are also cases in which a change increases expenditure without demonstrating a benefit in the selected outcome. The choice therefore requires examining both resource and performance measures; there is no general requirement that the most complex architecture, largest context or highest budget be preferred.

Fan et al. (2025) make this evaluation problem explicit in SWE-Effi, which considers issue resolution under token, monetary and time constraints. In their stratified sample of 50 SWE-bench Verified issues, Agentless with Qwen3-32B achieved 48% resolution and a token-budget effectiveness score of 46.7%, but averaged 727.9 seconds of local CPU time. AutoCodeRover with the same model achieved 38% resolution, a token-budget effectiveness score of 37.1% and 34.5 seconds of local CPU time. The contrast demonstrates that a method preferred under a model-resource objective may be less attractive under a local execution-time objective. These metrics are not interchangeable with elapsed developer lead time or enterprise gateway charges.

One implication of the evidence is that a lightweight workflow may be a reasonable option for tasks whose needs can be satisfied without extensive additional coordination, while tasks requiring prolonged investigation or state preservation may benefit from further structure. The cited studies do not establish a monotonic relationship between human-estimated complexity and orchestration benefit, nor identify a threshold at which additional orchestration becomes worthwhile. Testing the relative performance of complete workflows across task-complexity categories remains relevant. This issue also connects resource use to the delivery and quality outcomes considered in Sections 2.2 and 2.3: model expenditure can only be interpreted meaningfully alongside what the development process achieves.
