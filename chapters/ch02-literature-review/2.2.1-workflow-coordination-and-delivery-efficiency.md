#### 2.2.1 Workflow Coordination and Delivery Efficiency

A coding agent may complete a local edit quickly, yet that edit is only part of delivering a development task. The developer still has to establish what the change should do, locate relevant code and dependencies, check the result against the requirement, and decide whether further work is needed. The activities may occur in different orders as new information emerges. Section 1.2.1 therefore raises a broader efficiency question than the speed of code generation: how much time does the developer-owned task take when coordination, checking, and correction are included? A faster individual action may contribute to shorter delivery time, but the relationship depends on the work surrounding that action.

Empirical accounts show that working with AI involves substantial activity beyond requesting code. Otten et al. (2025) surveyed 91 software engineers, including 72 active users of generative AI, and found a preference for iterative, multi-turn interaction; respondents also reported greater difficulty with complex code generation and debugging than with documentation tasks. In a workplace shadowing study, Chou et al. (2026) observed 11 professional developers using coding agents on their own work. Formulating prompts and checking agent responses occupied an average of 33.4% of the observed session time. This figure describes how the developers spent their time, rather than time that could necessarily be eliminated. In a separate analysis of 20,574 real-world coding-agent sessions, Tang et al. (2026) documented developer-visible disagreements about project understanding, user intent, constraints, and agent actions. Among the misalignment episodes for which a resolution was visible, most involved explicit user correction. These studies give concrete reasons to examine the developer's role in directing and checking an agent, although neither measures how much of a task's delivery time these activities add.

Variation during agent execution introduces another possible source of coordination and correction. Bjarnason et al. (2026) repeated coding-agent runs on SWE-bench Verified and found that trajectories diverged early and could lead to different strategies and outcomes, even under settings with a sampling temperature of zero. Bouzenia and Pradel (2025), analysing 120 software-engineering agent trajectories, found that unsuccessful runs could contain repeated fix-generation, exploration, or testing actions. A varying path is not itself a failure, and a repeated action may be warranted during debugging. Nevertheless, when an agent's actions do not satisfy a task's requirements, the developer may need to investigate the result, redirect the work, or repeat a check. The cited studies establish variation and recurring failure patterns; they do not isolate randomness as the cause of developer rework or quantify its effect on delivery lead time.

The baseline in this study uses Claude Code's native agent capabilities. It can inspect a repository, plan, edit files, run commands, and use test feedback; developers can also provide project instructions and guide its next steps. The question is therefore whether organising these available activities through additional workflow rules changes the time taken to complete development tasks. An explicit sequence or checkpoint might prevent an overlooked step or make an unsuccessful attempt easier to diagnose. It might also add preparation and validation time to a task that the developer and agent could otherwise finish quickly. Both possibilities matter when assessing delivery efficiency, and neither follows automatically from the existence of a structured workflow.
