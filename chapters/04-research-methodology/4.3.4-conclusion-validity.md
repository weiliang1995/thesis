#### 4.3.4 Conclusion Validity

The number of eligible feature-development tasks may be limited or unevenly distributed between workflow conditions and complexity categories. Two developers contribute multiple tasks each, so the observations are not independent replications from a large population of developers. Reporting overall results without considering developer and complexity distributions could also create misleading impressions. The analysis therefore presents sample sizes, descriptive statistics, and observed differences for the overall groups and the available strata. Where a subgroup contains few tasks, differences are interpreted cautiously rather than treated as robust estimates.

Task records are assembled from a consolidated AI-development dashboard and branch-linked E2E reports, using Jira, GitLab, and gateway information. Incorrect issue-to-branch matches, missing reports, incomplete token records, or duplicate linked defects could affect the calculated measures. The data-preparation procedure checks these relationships and reports usable task counts separately for each metric. Missing values are not replaced with zero, and ratios with zero denominators are left undefined. These precautions improve transparency but cannot fully correct inaccurate or unavailable source records.

Unusually high defect-density values are retained when the underlying records are valid, because a small code change can still introduce a genuine defect. The study reports raw counts and change volumes alongside the ratios, rather than removing extreme outcomes merely because they influence the group average. The analysis also examines E2E correction-loop counts as supplementary evidence but does not infer a specific agent failure mechanism from an instruction-limit breach.

The study emphasizes the magnitude, direction, and consistency of observed differences, including trade-offs among lead time, software quality, and LLM cost. It does not assume that a conventional statistical test is suitable before task counts, distributions, and repeated observations have been examined. As the work is non-randomized and limited to two developers, the findings support context-specific comparisons, not a definitive causal claim or a general estimate of workflow effectiveness across software engineering teams.
